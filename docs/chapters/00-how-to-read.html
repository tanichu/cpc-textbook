2.1 Who this book is for

This book is written first for graduate students and researchers in informatics, cognitive science, and robotics. If you have met variational Bayes and Markov chain Monte Carlo before, even briefly, you have what you need; the appendices supply the rest.

It is written second, and deliberately, for graduate students in linguistics, anthropology, sociology, science studies, and economics. That second audience is not an afterthought. The central claim of this book is that symbol emergence systems are a single kind of object, and that the mathematics of that object does not care whether the symbols in question are words, prices, scientific concepts, or names for feelings. If that claim is right, then the people who study those systems from the outside should be able to reach the mathematics. Making that reachable is a design problem for the book, not a matter of goodwill.

I have not solved that problem by lowering the mathematical level. The equations are the content, and softening them would leave nothing behind. I have tried to solve it structurally: by front-loading the core, by making every later chapter a marked difference from that core, by opening each chapter with a paragraph that states its claim without symbols, and by providing a glossary that maps the vocabulary of the humanities and social sciences onto the variables of the model.

2.2 Reading paths

If you want the argument without the derivations, read:

Chapters 1 and 2 → Chapter 13 → Chapter 24 → Part VI → Chapter 31

That is roughly a hundred pages. It will give you the problem, the core formulation, the connection to predictive coding and the free energy principle, the layered picture of cognition across timescales, the isomorphic systems, and the open problems. You will not have seen why the core formulation is the right one, and you will have to take the theorems on trust, but you will not be missing the point.

I want to be explicit that this is a legitimate way to read the book, not a concession. Readers skip; the only question is whether they skip with a map or without one. Everything in Parts I through V is there because someone will want it, and no chapter in those parts is a prerequisite for the path above.

If you want the mathematics, read Part I onward in order. Part I earns the formulation of Chapter 2, Part II supplies the algorithms, Part III the energetics, Part IV action and multiple agents, Part V the properties of the resulting systems.

If you came from a specific application — from economics, from science studies, from affective science — go to Part VI first and work backwards when a chapter stops making sense. Part VI is written so that it does not depend on Part II. This costs some repetition, which I consider a fair price.

2.3 How the book is built

One chapter, one proposition. Most chapters are organized around a single claim, and most of those claims have the same shape: language game G is a correct inference algorithm for the posterior of generative model \mathcal{M}. The theory presented here is not a narrative but the collection of these propositions. Where a chapter has no such proposition, it is doing preparatory or interpretive work, and says so.

Chapters 1 and 2 carry the core. Chapter 1 states the problem and the two constraints that every later chapter must respect. Chapter 2 gives the generative model and the collective posterior it induces. Everything after Chapter 2 can be read as a commentary on Chapter 2.

Extensions are shown as differences. When a later chapter extends the model, it does so by modifying the equation of Chapter 2 in a stated way: put w_{1:T} in place of w; multiply in a term for the optimality variable; let w range over a continuous space. The same equation returns at the head of each such chapter, so that it is always clear what has changed and what has not. This is why the book can afford to be long without becoming hard to navigate.

Each chapter opens with a summary in words. One paragraph, no symbols, stating what the chapter claims. These are not decoration. If you read only those paragraphs you will have a correct, if thin, account of the whole theory.

Mathematical preparation is in the appendices. Appendix A covers variational inference and Appendix B covers Markov chain Monte Carlo. Neither is a substitute for a course, and both are there so that the main text does not have to stop and explain. Appendix C fixes notation. Appendix D is the glossary between the humanities and social sciences and the mathematics. Appendix E is a guide to the primary literature, organized by where you are coming from.

2.4 A word about the state of this text

This book is being written in the open, and it is not finished. Nothing in it should be cited yet.

Each chapter carries two independent marks, one for how much of it has been written and one for how far it has been checked. They are kept separate on purpose. Drafting can now run far ahead of verification, and a single combined percentage would hide exactly the gap that matters. A chapter can be complete in length and still be unverified prose; you are entitled to know which.

2.5 A note on scope

This book is a mathematical theory of symbol emergence systems. It is not a survey of symbol emergence in robotics, and I have avoided reproducing the structure of the earlier survey literature. It is also not an introduction to the free energy principle, though Chapter 13 explains the relationship. The mathematics of Parts I and II closes with variational inference and MCMC alone, and does not depend on whether the free energy principle is true.

Corrections and objections are welcome, in English or Japanese, while the book is still in draft. That is the reason it is in the open.
